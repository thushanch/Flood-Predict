## 1. Operational definitions of flash floods (WMO, NWS, Gaume, Borga, Marchi)

### Takeaway
The only widely used operational definition is time-based: the US NWS puts flood onset within 6 h of the causative event. The European school (Gaume, Borga, Marchi) adds scale and process criteria: small catchments (less than about 500–1000 km²), convective or orographic rain, and response times of a few hours. In practice these are combined with a unit-peak-discharge severity threshold. No single global, quantitative definition exists.

### Cited Findings
- NWS glossary: a flash flood is "a rapid and extreme flow of high water into a normally dry area, or a rapid water level rise in a stream or creek above a predetermined flood level, beginning within six hours of the causative event (e.g., intense rainfall, dam failure, ice jam)". The glossary also says the time threshold "may vary in different parts of the country". — [NWS Glossary](https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=flash)
- Kelsch (COMET/AMS) notes that the NWS definition rests on the time lag from causative rainfall to flooding (6 h or less). Rain amount and stream response cannot define a flash flood because both vary widely, and many flash floods occur less than 2 h after the rain, so 6 h is a ceiling. — [Kelsch, AMS conference paper](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/31246.pdf); [Kelsch, AMS paper 2](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/58828.pdf)
- AMS Glossary: a flash flood occurs "generally within minutes to several hours of the rainfall event". Causes also include ice jams and levee or dam failure. — [AMS Glossary of Meteorology](https://glossary.ametsoc.org/wiki/flash-flood/)
- Gaume et al. (2009, J. Hydrol. 367:70–78, doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2008.12.028, confirmed) compiled more than 550 events across seven European hydrometeorological regions. They focused on estimating peak discharge ranges for catchments **under 500 km²**. Mediterranean extremes are larger than inland continental ones, and occurrence is strongly seasonal with region-specific climatic drivers. — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00400570); [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/1471790)
- Marchi et al. (2010, J. Hydrol. 394(1–2):118–133) studied 25 extreme flash floods in 60 basins of **9.5–1856 km²**, mostly in a belt from the western Mediterranean to the Black Sea. Events were selected for high rainfall intensity, strong flood response and reliable high-resolution data. More than 50% of peak discharges came from **post-flood surveys in ungauged streams**. Mediterranean and Alpine-Mediterranean events cluster in autumn, inland Continental events in summer. — [Univ. Padova record](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2422543); [BibBase](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/marchi-borga-preciso-gaume-characterisationofselectedextremeflashfloodsineuropeandimplicationsforfloodriskmanagement)
- Borga et al. (2011, Environ. Sci. Policy 14(7):834–844, doi:10.1016/j.envsci.2011.05.017, confirmed), the HYDRATE project paper: flash floods develop at space and time scales that conventional rain and discharge monitoring cannot capture. Seasonality, spatial extent and runoff coefficients differ markedly across European climatic regions. HYDRATE (FP6 grant 037024, 2006–2010) aimed to improve forecasting **in ungauged basins** and produced a European Flash Flood Database. — [TU Wien project page](https://repositum.tuwien.at/cris/project/pj03633); [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/37024); [Climate-ADAPT](https://climate-adapt.eea.europa.eu/en/metadata/projects/hydrometeorological-data-resources-and-technologies-for-effective-flash-flood-forecasting)
- Borga, Stoffel, Marchi, Marra & Jakob (2014, J. Hydrol. 518:194–205, doi:10.1016/j.jhydrol.2014.05.022, confirmed) review flash floods and debris flows in headwater systems. These processes occur at scales conventional observation systems cannot monitor, so their controls are poorly understood. Research priorities: rainfall estimation and nowcasting at the relevant scales; consolidating physical, engineering and social datasets of events; integrated multi-hazard warning. — [CNR-IRPI record](https://www.irpi.cnr.it/publication/hydrogeomorphic-response-to-extreme-rainfall-in-headwater-systems_-flash-floods-and-debris-flows)

### Inferences
- A Sri Lankan operational definition should combine (a) the NWS-style onset lag (6 h or less, possibly shorter for wet-zone hill catchments), (b) a catchment-area ceiling consistent with the European literature (about 500–1000 km²), and (c) a unit-peak-discharge or rise-rate severity criterion (see Sections 3 and 6). Under such a definition the slow lowland floods of the Kelani, Kalu and Mahaweli main stems would count as riverine floods, while their steep upland tributaries could qualify as flash-flood catchments.
- The Marchi et al. finding that more than half the European peak data came from post-flood surveys is a strong methodological precedent for Sri Lanka, where small streams are largely ungauged.
- Distinguishing flash from riverine floods: in this literature the distinction rests on response time relative to rainfall duration, small spatial scale, convective or orographic forcing, high unit peak discharge and frequent co-occurrence with debris flows and landslides (Borga 2014). Riverine floods are large-basin, long-duration, multi-day-rainfall events.

### Gaps
- **WMO definition not retrieved.** [UNVERIFIED] The WMO International Glossary of Hydrology and WMO flash-flood guidance documents describe a flash flood as a flood "of short duration with a relatively high peak discharge", typically onset within about 6 h. - [UNVERIFIED] Georgakakos (2006, J. Hydrol.) on analytical flash flood guidance is the basis of the WMO/HRC FFGS. Sri Lanka is reportedly part of the South Asia FFGS (SAsiaFFGS) regional system. Confirm coverage and status with WMO and the Sri Lanka Department of Meteorology.
- Gaume 2009's exact definitional thresholds (e.g., any unit-peak cut-off) could not be verified from the full text.

## 2. Flash Flood Potential Index (FFPI): Smith (2003) and later applications

### Takeaway
The FFPI is a static, GIS-based index. It reclassifies slope, land use/cover, soil texture/permeability and vegetation/forest density onto a 1–10 scale and combines them, originally as an equal-weight mean. Applications have spread from NWS offices to Romania, Malaysia, Indonesia, India and elsewhere. The main lines of development are re-weighting (expert or AHP weights, then statistical and machine-learning weights trained on inventories of observed torrential events). Its main weaknesses are subjective weights and weak validation.

### Cited Findings
- Origin: Greg Smith (NWS Colorado Basin RFC, 2003), white paper "Flash Flood Potential: Determining the Hydrologic Response of FFMP Basins to Heavy Rain by Analyzing Their Physiographic Characteristics". It was developed as a supplement to the Flash Flood Monitoring and Prediction (FFMP) system. — [Ceru, ESRI Fed UC 2012](https://proceedings.esri.com/library/userconf/feduc12/papers/user/joeceru.pdf); [Kruzdlo, NWS presentation (DRBC)](https://nj.gov/drbc/library/documents/Flood_Website/flood-warning/user-forums/Krudzlo_NWS.pdf)
- NOAA describes the FFPI as quantifying "a given sub-basin's risk of flash flooding based on its inherent, static characteristics such as slope, land cover, land use and soil type/texture". — [NOAA FFPI dataset (WIFIRE catalogue)](https://wifire-data.sdsc.edu/dataset/natural-hazards-flash-flood-potential-index-noaa)
- Kruzdlo (NWS Mount Holly) and Ceru built versions for the Mount Holly/Philadelphia and Pennsylvania areas using Smith's equal weighting. In 2009 Brewster (NWS Binghamton) modified it to give **greater weight to slope and vegetation cover** than to land use and soil. — [Ceru 2012](https://proceedings.esri.com/library/userconf/feduc12/papers/user/joeceru.pdf)
- Applications elsewhere: Shah Alam, Malaysia (FFPI as a dimensionless 1–10 index with scenarios) — [Planning Malaysia J.](https://www.planningmalaysia.org/index.php/pmj/article/view/1255). Indonesia (classes: 2–3.5 low, 3.5–5 moderate, more than 5 high; thresholds specific to one study) — [J. Teknik Sipil (UTP)](https://ejournal.utp.ac.id/index.php/JTSA/article/download/5925/520523463). Several authors modified FFPI to reduce subjectivity in weighting — [Open Geosciences (De Gruyter) 2018](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/geo-2018-0047/html).
- India: a 2024 study of the Tawi basin used a **Modified FFPI (MFFPI)** to map areas predisposed to water accumulation. — [Geosystems and Geoenvironment 2024 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/185858904d8e4e3e8d0a30e07242a642)
- Romania (Costache school): Minea (2013) applied physiographic FFPI to the Bâsca catchment. Costache & Zaharia did a first statistical (bivariate) weighting of FFPI predictors on Bâsca Chiojdului. Later work moved to ensembles: AHP / kNN / K-Star (2019); DNN-AHP and DNN-FR (2020, accuracy above 92%); XGBoost, RF and BRT (2021); and a 2021 *Sensors* paper combining DLNN and alternating decision trees with frequency-ratio or weights-of-evidence, trained on 481 torrential-process points plus 481 random absence points (best: DLNN-WOE, K-index 0.97). — [Costache et al. 2021, Sensors (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7796316/); [Crossref record doi:10.3390/s21010280](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390%2FS21010280); [MDPI Water 12(6):1549](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/12/6/1549/xml); [MDPI Water 13(6):758](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/13/6/758)
- Sri Lanka: no FFPI-by-name study was found. The nearest is a 2014 GIS weighted-overlay "flooding potential" model for the upper Gal Oya basin. — [KALAM (SEUSL) 2014](https://www.seu.ac.lk/researchandpublications/kalam/vol8/Pages%20from%20KALAM%20VIII%20(1)%202014%20%20(Page%201-9.pdf). Nepal (East Rapti) used morphometric prioritization rather than FFPI. — [ISPRS IJGI 2021 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/4f25b19eb4c54602a6636ba98f8fbbe7)

### Inferences
- The method has evolved in three stages: (1) equal-weight physiographic index (Smith 2003); (2) expert or AHP re-weighting (Brewster 2009, many Iran, Turkey, Saudi Arabia and India GIS-MCDA papers); (3) data-driven weights, where FFPI becomes a susceptibility model trained on presence/absence inventories with FR, WoE, RF, XGBoost or DNN (Costache and co-authors). Stage 3 needs an event inventory, which links Section 2 to Section 5.
- Validation in most FFPI papers is ROC/AUC against inventory points, usually the same kind of data the model was trained on. Few papers validate against hydrological response (e.g., observed flashiness or unit peak discharge). This is a clear gap a Sri Lankan study could fill by checking FFPI against gauged flashiness (e.g., Irrigation Department gauges).
- The FFPI's inputs (DEM slope, land cover, soil texture, NDVI or forest cover) are globally available (SRTM/Copernicus DEM, ESA WorldCover, SoilGrids, Sentinel-2), so the method transfers readily to data-scarce tropics. Its static nature, however, ignores antecedent moisture, which matters greatly under monsoon regimes (see the India drivers finding in Section 4).

### Gaps
- Smith (2003) full text was not retrieved. [UNVERIFIED] The original FFPI is commonly written as FFPI = (M + L + S + V)/N: slope (M), land use (L), soil (S) and vegetation (V) each rescaled 1–10, with slope sometimes weighted higher (e.g., ×1.5 or ×2 in Kruzdlo-type variants). - No published critique dedicated to FFPI was found. Criticisms here (subjective weights, static inputs, circular validation) are synthesized from the applied literature and are inference, not a cited critique.

## 3. Flashiness indices: R-B index, Saharia flashiness, Holko, time-to-peak, rise rate; continental maps

### Takeaway
Two families exist. (1) **Regime indices** from daily flows, mainly the Richards-Baker Flashiness Index (RBI), which is easy to compute from Sri Lankan daily gauge records. (2) **Event-scale severity metrics** from sub-daily hydrographs, such as Saharia et al.'s (2017) flashiness: rise rate of the rising limb normalized by area. Saharia et al. mapped event flashiness across the CONUS by regressing it on geomorphic and climatic predictors, which is the template for regionalizing it to ungauged basins.

### Cited Findings
- Baker, Richards, Loftus & Kramer (2004), "A new flashiness index: characteristics and applications to Midwestern rivers and streams" (JAWRA). The R-B index is the sum of absolute day-to-day changes in daily mean discharge divided by the sum of daily mean discharge. It is dimensionless and comparable across rivers. — [MN Water Research Digital Library](https://wrl.mnpals.net/taxonomy/term/7373); [Michigan DEQ R-B application (308 USGS gages)](https://www.michigan.gov/documents/deq/wrd-hsdsu-flashiness_386624_7.pdf)
- RBI depends on watershed size: smaller watersheds are flashier (US analysis of 1,144 watersheds). Holko et al. (2011) found that the RBI–catchment area relationship **varies by hydroclimatic region** (Slovakia/Austria mountain streams). — [Frontiers in Environ. Sci. 2023 (Hornád, Slovakia)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1203965/pdf)
- Drivers and buffers of stream flashiness have been studied regionally, e.g., in the US Mid-Atlantic. — [EBSCO record](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/agriculture-and-agribusiness/61e1d333-dd01-5a5e-a390-e1e6837e33de/drivers-and-buffers-of-stream-flashiness-in-the-mid-atlantic-united-states/)
- Saharia, Kirstetter, Vergara, Gourley, Hong & Giroud (2017), "Mapping flash flood severity in the United States", J. Hydrometeorol. 18(2):397–411, doi:10.1175/JHM-D-16-0082.1 (confirmed):
  - "Flashiness" is defined as the rate of rise of the hydrograph during flooding conditions, capturing both magnitude and timing, from the slope of the rising limb.
  - They used a 78-year archive of events combining USGS gauge records with NWS flood thresholds.
  - Flashiness at gauged basins was modelled as a function of geomorphological and climatological variables and extended to a high-resolution CONUS grid.
  - They identified **six flash-flood hotspots** and examined seasonality, then compared results with NWS Storm Reports and a flood-fatalities database.
  - Sources: [NOAA IR record](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/48334); [Univ. Iowa record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Mapping-Flash-Flood-Severity-in-the/9984446426702771); [AGU FM15 abstract](https://agu.confex.com/agu/fm15/webprogram/Paper83577.html); [Saharia PhD thesis, OU: "Characterization and Prediction of Flash Flood Severity"](https://shareok.org/items/7b0a6de9-3392-4a45-8724-6b0dce78fb83/full)
- Li, Gourley, Hong et al. (2021, ESSD 13:3755) built a 120-year US flood database, extending Gourley et al. (2013). — [ESSD 2021](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/13/3755/2021/essd-13-3755-2021.html)
- Indian subcontinent: Nandana, Vegad & Mishra (2025, npj Natural Hazards, doi:10.1038/s44304-025-00121-3) mapped flash-flood-prone sub-basins from hydrological and geomorphological characteristics. — [IAS repository](https://repository.ias.ac.in/142483). Secondary press reports (not verified against the paper) say hotspots are concentrated in the Himalaya, the **west coast (Western Ghats)** and Central India. About 75% of flash floods were reported to arise from extreme rain on saturated soils and about 25% from extreme rain alone. — [Vision IAS summary](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/news-today/2025-07-30/environment/new-study-maps-flash-flood-prone-hotspots-across-the-indian-subcontinent); [Vajiram & Ravi](https://vajiramandravi.com/current-affairs/?p=57343)

### Inferences
- For Sri Lanka, the RBI is computable today from Irrigation Department daily discharge. Saharia-type rise-rate flashiness needs sub-daily stage, available only at a few stations (e.g., telemetry stations installed since around 2018 under the CRIP/DSWRPP programmes [UNVERIFIED]). A practical design is to compute RBI widely and event flashiness where sub-daily data exist, then regionalize both.
- The finding that RBI scaling with area varies by hydroclimatic region (Holko) implies a separate regional relationship for Sri Lanka's wet, intermediate and dry zones, rather than transferring US curves.
- The India result, that antecedent saturation drives most flash floods, supports adding dynamic antecedent-moisture predictors to static flashiness or FFPI regionalization in monsoon climates.

Also unverified: the regression or ML method (multiple regression vs. random forest), the event count, and the hotspot names. The full text could not be fetched; verify via doi:10.1175/JHM-D-16-0082.1.

## 4. Regionalization of flash flood response to ungauged catchments

### Takeaway
Three families dominate: (a) regression or ML of a response metric (flashiness, unit peak, FFPI weights) on catchment descriptors (Saharia 2017; Costache ensembles); (b) similarity, proximity or regional-envelope transfer (European envelope curves, PUB literature); (c) uncalibrated or a-priori-parameterized distributed models driven by radar or satellite QPE (CREST/EF5 in the US FLASH system; WMO FFGS globally). Option (c) with a-priori parameters is the most transferable to data-scarce tropics.

### Cited Findings
- EF5 (Flamig, Vergara & Gourley 2020, GMD 13:4943–4958, doi:10.5194/gmd-13-4943-2020, confirmed) contains several water-balance models (CREST, SAC-SMA, a hydrophobic end-member) and two routing schemes. It produces ensemble streamflow, **unit streamflow** (discharge per upstream area) and soil saturation. In tests on basins **under 1,000 km²** across CONUS, the **uncalibrated** water-balance models coupled to kinematic-wave routing were skillful. EF5 powers the FLASH system, transitioned to NWS operations in November 2016. Parameter data (doi:10.5281/zenodo.4009759) and code (doi:10.5281/zenodo.569078) are open. — [GMD paper](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/13/4943/2020/); [EF5/CREST parameter explorer](https://skyan1002-ef5-crest-us-parameter-explorer.static.hf.space/index.html)
- Saharia et al. (2017) regionalized gauge-based flashiness to a CONUS grid via geomorphological and climatological predictors (see Section 3). — [NOAA IR record](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/48334)
- The HYDRATE project's explicit final goal was strengthening flash flood forecasting in **ungauged basins**. — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/37024); [TU Wien](https://repositum.tuwien.at/cris/project/pj03633)
- ML regionalization of FFPI-type susceptibility (DLNN, ADT, RF, XGBoost, BRT) trained on observed event points is well established in Romania (Section 2). — [Costache et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7796316/)

### Inferences
- For Sri Lanka, a credible hybrid would combine: (1) RBI and unit-peak metrics at gauges, regionalized with random forest or gradient boosting on DEM, soil, land-cover and rainfall-climatology descriptors (Saharia template); (2) an uncalibrated EF5/CREST setup driven by IMERG or GSMaP to produce unit-streamflow thresholds at ungauged outlets (FLASH template); (3) FFPI as a static susceptibility layer validated against (1) and an event inventory.

(2017, BAMS) "The FLASH project"; Merz & Blöschl (2003, WRR) process typology of floods; Blöschl et al. (2013) *Runoff Prediction in Ungauged Basins* (Cambridge UP); Hrachowitz et al. ## 5. Event inventories: EU, US, China, Mediterranean, global, and data-scarce approaches

### Takeaway
High-quality flash-flood inventories are hydrometeorological and survey-based: the European HYDRATE/EuroMedeFF datasets and China's national investigation. Large impact databases (NWS Storm Events, EM-DAT, DFO, DesInventar) and satellite inundation databases (Global Flood Database) are much less specific to flash floods. In data-scarce regions inventories are increasingly built by mining news text with ML or LLMs, digitizing national meteorological records (India Flood Inventory) and post-event surveys.

### Cited Findings
- **Europe:** Gaume et al. (2009), more than 550 events in seven regions. — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00400570). Marchi et al. (2010), 25 extreme events in 60 basins. — [Padova](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2422543). HYDRATE produced a freely accessible European Flash Flood Database. — [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/37024)
- **EuroMedeFF:** Amponsah et al. (2018, ESSD 10:1783–1794, doi:10.5194/essd-10-1783-2018, confirmed) is an integrated high-resolution dataset of high-intensity European and Mediterranean flash floods over 1991–2015. The preprint describes **49 events** in France, Israel, Germany, Slovenia, Romania and Italy. Data are public (doi:10.6096/MISTRALS-HyMeX.1493). — [ESSD article PDF](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/10/1783/2018/essd-10-1783-2018.pdf); [ESSD preprint](https://essd.copernicus.org/preprints/essd-2018-42/essd-2018-42.pdf)
- **USA:** Gourley et al. (2013, BAMS 94(6):799–805, doi:10.1175/BAMS-D-12-00198.1, confirmed), the Unified Flash Flood Database. It was the first georeferenced US flash-flood database assembled and reprocessed from three sources: USGS automated discharge records, NWS flash flood reports (Storm Data) and SHAVE public witness reports (2008–2010). Intended uses include evaluating prediction tools and characterizing regional and seasonal trends. — [USGS publication](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70048422); [HAL](https://dumas.ccsd.cnrs.fr/IRSTEA/hal-03091825v1). Extended in Li et al. (2021), a 120-year US flood database. — [ESSD 2021](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/13/3755/2021/essd-13-3755-2021.html)
- **China:** the national flash flood investigation and evaluation project lasted 4 years and covered 30 provinces, 305 cities and 2,138 counties. It used census, on-site investigation, field measurement, hydrological analysis and evaluation, and is described as the largest non-engineering water-sector project since 1949. A 2019 NHESS preprint built a 60-year event dataset from it (held by IWHR). Public access to the raw database is unclear. — [NHESS preprint nhess-2019-150](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2019-150/nhess-2019-150.pdf); [J. Geo-information Science](https://www.dqxxkx.cn/EN/abstract/article/1560-8999/39845). Caveat: Chinese pre-2018 disaster-report web data became inaccessible after the Ministry of Emergency Management was created, and yearbooks record only severe events. — [HESS 29:767 (2025)](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/767/2025/)
- **Global Flood Database:** Tellman et al. (2021, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-021-03695-w, confirmed) mapped **913 events** (2000–2018) in 169 countries from 12,719 MODIS Terra/Aqua scenes. Events were selected from the Dartmouth Flood Observatory catalogue and give maximum extent and duration at 250 m. — [Google Earth Engine catalogue](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GLOBAL_FLOOD_DB_MODIS_EVENTS_V1); [WEF summary](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2021/09/millions-more-people-have-been-exposed-to-floods-since-2000/)
- **India Flood Inventory (IFI):** Saharia et al. (2021, Natural Hazards 108:619–633) covers 1985–2016. About 89% of records come from digitized IMD "Disastrous Weather Events" reports, the rest from EM-DAT and DFO, with GIS geocoding. It has since been extended to IFI-Impacts (1967–2023), with district flooded area (v2) and a District Flood Severity Index (v4). — [UCAR impacts record](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/india-flood-inventory-creation-of-a-multi-source-national-geospat/); [Mongabay India](https://india.mongabay.com/2021/11/freely-available-geospatial-dataset-to-make-flood-research-easier/); [Zenodo IFI-Impacts v4](https://zenodo.org/records/16994648). **INDOFLOODS** combines station discharge with official warning and danger levels and catchment attributes. — [Météo-France library record](https://bibliotheque.meteo.fr/pub/DOC00060472-indofloods-comprehensive-database-for-flood-events.html)
- **News-mining inventories (data-scarce):**
  - Bangladesh: a 40,000-article flood news dataset from 10 outlets. Context-aware classifiers beat keyword matching. The news-derived series correlated with satellite inundation (Spearman ρ = 0.70) and outperformed Twitter-derived series against EM-DAT and satellite data (arXiv preprint). — [arXiv 2312.14943](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.14943)
  - Denmark: manual review of a 75-million-article media archive yielded 1,118 flooded locations (2007–2020). — [EGU24-16370](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU24/EGU24-16370.html)
  - "Groundsource" (EarthArXiv preprint, March 2026, not peer reviewed): about 2.6 million flood events automatically extracted from more than 5 million news articles in over 150 countries. — [GEE community catalogue entry](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/groundsource/)
  - Caveat: news-based records undercount floods in low-GDP areas, and language-specific methods may not transfer to the Global South. — [HESS-2024-146 reviewer comments](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2024-146/hess-2024-146-AC3-supplement.pdf)
  - Newspaper reports have been used to validate flood-risk maps in sparsely monitored areas. — [PreventionWeb/Eos](https://www.preventionweb.net/news/can-newspaper-reporting-uncover-flood-risk)

### Inferences
- A Sri Lankan inventory could follow the IFI template: digitize Department of Meteorology and DMC situation reports and DesInventar Sri Lanka records, then merge with EM-DAT, DFO and GFD footprints. ## 6. Controls on flash flood magnitude: area scaling, envelope curves, soils, slope, land use

### Takeaway
Unit peak discharge decreases with catchment area as a power law. Amponsah et al. (2020) found the **envelope-curve exponent is nearly invariant across European and Mediterranean climatic regions, while the multiplier varies by region**. This is directly useful for building a Sri Lankan regional envelope from a limited post-event dataset. Flashiness (RBI) also declines with area, but the relationship differs by hydroclimatic region. Slope, soil permeability, land use and antecedent saturation modulate the response.

### Cited Findings
- Amponsah, Marra, Marchi, Roux, Braud & Borga (2020) used quantile regression on 1991–2015 European and Mediterranean flash floods to define regional envelope curves of unit peak discharge vs. drainage area. "The exponent of the envelope curves shows almost negligible variations among climatic regions whereas the multiplier depends on the climatic regions." — [CNR-IRPI record](https://www.irpi.cnr.it/publication/objective-analysis-of-envelope-curves-for-peak-floods-of-european-and-mediterranean-flash-floods)
- Gaume et al. (2009): extreme flash floods (catchments under 500 km²) are larger in Mediterranean than in inland continental regions. — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00400570)
- A comparison of flash-flood regimes in the north-western vs. south-eastern Mediterranean was published in NHESS 12:1255 (2012). — [NHESS 12:1255](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/12/1255/2012/nhess-12-1255-2012.html)
- RBI decreases with watershed size (1,144 US watersheds), with region-dependent scaling (Holko et al. 2011). — [Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1203965/pdf)
- Borga et al. (2011): runoff coefficients and other flash-flood properties differ markedly across European climatic regions. — [TU Wien HYDRATE](https://repositum.tuwien.at/cris/project/pj03633)
- FFPI practice treats slope, land use, soil texture and vegetation as the primary static controls, with slope and vegetation often weighted highest (Brewster 2009). — [Ceru 2012](https://proceedings.esri.com/library/userconf/feduc12/papers/user/joeceru.pdf)
- India: extreme rain on saturated soils is reported as the dominant flash-flood driver (about 75%). This comes from secondary reports, not verified against the paper. — [Vision IAS](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/news-today/2025-07-30/environment/new-study-maps-flash-flood-prone-hotspots-across-the-indian-subcontinent)

### Inferences
- A practical recipe for Sri Lanka is to fix the European envelope exponent (or estimate it from pooled tropical data) and fit only the regional multiplier from a small set of surveyed or gauged extremes. This is sample-efficient and well suited to data scarcity. Checking whether Sri Lankan wet-zone extremes plot above the Mediterranean envelope would be a novel, publishable result.
- Static controls (slope, soils, land use) explain spatial susceptibility. Event magnitude in monsoon climates is also strongly governed by antecedent wetness (India finding), which argues for including dynamic soil-moisture predictors (e.g., SMAP, ERA5-Land) in any Sri Lankan regionalization.
