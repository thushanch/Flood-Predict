# Storm shape can sharpen Sri Lankan flash-flood thresholds

Flash flood assessment for Sri Lanka is worth researching, and most of it is still open. Across the susceptibility, modelling, flood frequency, early warning and rainfall literatures, the notes found **no peer-reviewed study that formally defines Sri Lankan flash floods, derives a rainfall trigger for them, maps catchment flashiness, or verifies the regional Flash Flood Guidance System (FFGS) over the island**. Sri Lankan flood research is basin-scale and riverine (Kelani, Kalu, Gin, Nilwala) and mostly uses daily data. The warning system is split three ways: river-stage flood warnings from the Irrigation Department, rainfall-amount advisories from the Department of Meteorology, and landslide rainfall thresholds from NBRO. Small, steep catchments that respond in 2–3 hours fall between all three. Global practice has moved to conditional rainfall thresholds, regionalized event-scale flashiness and survey- or news-based event inventories. Even internationally, though, one question has not been tested on a large observed event sample: does the shape of the storm hyetograph separate flood from non-flood events, or improve threshold skill, beyond depth and duration? The notes found no such study. **For this researcher, the strongest direction merges candidates 1 and 3 into one study: shape-conditioned empirical rainfall triggering thresholds for small steep wet-zone catchments.** It would build on the researcher's existing Huff/Gini pipeline, use an event inventory compiled from DesInventar and DMC records, take rainfall from hourly gauges and IMERG, and score skill against depth–duration baselines and the 100 mm/24 h benchmark that NBRO and DoM already use. A national flashiness map (candidate 2) is the best second paper. Regional and non-stationary flood frequency analysis (candidate 4) fills a real gap but fits flash floods least well. The main risks are imprecise event timing in the inventory, access to hourly gauge data, and IMERG's negative bias and 1–4 h timing errors in the wet zone. Two caveats apply throughout. First, almost all evidence came from abstracts, repository records and search snippets, because publisher full texts could not be opened, so every citation below must be checked before it goes into a manuscript; items the notes flagged as unverified or from prior knowledge are marked **[unverified]** or **[prior knowledge]**. Second, Cyclone Ditwah (November 2025) is excluded throughout.

## Sri Lankan flood science stops at basin scale and daily resolution

### Susceptibility mapping is thin, riverine and untested in the uplands

Data-driven flood susceptibility mapping in Sri Lanka comes down to a handful of studies. The only machine-learning study found is Kurugama et al. (2024) in the upper Kalu around Ratnapura. It ran five boosting algorithms on a DMC/satellite/field inventory, and **LightGBM performed best (AUC 0.970, F1 0.907)**. Transferred outside the training region, that same LightGBM model fell to AUC 0.800, which the authors call "moderate" performance. They also state that ML applications to Sri Lankan flood susceptibility are "limited" ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jfr3.12980)). One snippet gives 445 flood locations and another 595 inventory points; this conflict is unverified. A logistic regression study of the Nilwala basin used four conditioning factors and 205 flood / 199 non-flood points, with AUC of 86% for success and 87% for prediction ([SLJOL](https://engineer.sljol.info/articles/10.4038/engineer.v55i2.7503)). A Kalutara logistic regression study reporting AUC 0.89 could not be re-located and should be treated as low-tier and unverified ([JournalsPub](https://journalspub.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/1-11-FLOOD-SUSCEPTIBILITY-MODELLING-USING-LOGISTIC-REGRESSION-IN-QGIS-ENVIRONMENT-1.pdf)).

The remaining studies are AHP/MCDA hazard or vulnerability overlays, mostly not validated against an inventory. They cover Neluwa in the upper Gin ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/15/6/1212)), Thudawa ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ids/ijidsc/v16y2024i1p90-108.html)), the lower Nilwala ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44177-026-00114-5)) and Colombo ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12665-026-12888-3)). The Thudawa paper's reported 72.2% ROC accuracy is unverified. The same paper calls a lowland river-mouth setting "flash flood" risk, which shows how loosely the term is used locally. **No Sri Lankan flood susceptibility study using frequency ratio, weights of evidence, entropy, SVM, ANN or deep learning was found, and none covers Kegalle, Badulla or Nuwara Eliya.** Shannon entropy appears only in a Kegalle landslide study ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40808-019-00615-w)).

### Models are well calibrated but almost never run hourly on small catchments

HEC-HMS, HEC-RAS and ICHARM's RRI model dominate. De Silva, Weerakoon and Herath (2014) calibrated event and continuous HEC-HMS models for the Kelani with an **average event NSE of 0.91** ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Modeling-of-Event-and-Continuous-Flow-Hydrographs-Silva-Weerakoon/5ae00035f0dfcb5b402a2167010c7270fb74f49b)). The WEB-RRI model reached Nash efficiency above 0.9 in the Kalu and Mundeni basins ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169419308984)). An RRI study of the Kalu found that **IMERG underestimated peak discharge relative to gauge forcing** ([ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2024MESE...10.1825H/abstract)).

The closest thing to a flash-flood event model is Dhanapala et al. (2022). They calibrated HEC-HMS with **hourly rainfall and streamflow** for three events above Pitabeddara (roughly 291–306 km²), coupled it to iRIC 2D, and found that loss parameters control peak magnitude while transform and routing parameters control timing ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2306-5338/9/2/17)). That study is framed as riverine flooding, sits near the upper limit of flash-flood catchment size, and should serve as the baseline citation for any small-catchment event model.

ML forecasting work is also daily. A Bi-LSTM study at Ratnapura forecasts 1–3 days ahead from 2014–2020 daily data, and it notes that the current real-time water-level app gives only a few hours' warning ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2571-9394/7/2/29)). The notes found no GR4J, TOPMODEL or HEC-RAS rain-on-grid flash-flood application in Sri Lanka. That could be a true absence or a search limitation. An apparent "flash flood forecasting for small rivers" paper in *JFRM* turned out to be Japanese, not Sri Lankan ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jfr3.70026)).

### Flood frequency analysis is at-site, stationary and relies on 1984 design methods

Published flood frequency analysis (FFA) is limited to a few at-site studies. Weerabangsa et al. (2023) fitted five distributions to a **44-year annual maximum series at Ratnapura**. GEV fitted best on the L-moment ratio diagram, and Mann-Kendall and Levene tests found no trend. The authors propose GEV as a candidate for future regional FFA ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/10355531/)). Dissanayake and Wickramaarachchi (2026) analysed ungauged Akuressa (Nilwala) using regionalised SWAT flows. **Three-parameter distributions fitted best**, and the 2003 and 2017 peaks plotted at roughly 250- and 333-year return periods ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40808-026-02856-y)). Two Kelani FFA papers exist, but their authors and venues are unverified ([Academia](https://www.academia.edu/40804290/Flood_Frequency_Analysis_for_Kelani_River_Basin_Case_Study_in_Sri_Lanka); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327745195_Flood_Frequency_Analysis_for_the_Kelani_River_and_the_Design_Flood_for_Kelani_Flood_Bunds)). **No regional (index-flood, L-moment homogeneity) FFA and no non-stationary FFA of Sri Lankan discharge was found.**

Small-catchment design still rests on Ponrajah's 1984 Irrigation Department manual. It provides island-wide IDF curves for six climatic zones and limits the Rational and Ponrajah methods to catchments under about 25 km² ([UoM catalogue](https://opac.lib.uom.lk/cgi-bin/koha/opac-detail.pl?biblionumber=139215&shelfbrowse_itemnumber=126320)). The choice of runoff-coefficient guideline produces large variation in Rational-method design floods ([SLJOL](https://engineer.sljol.info/articles/10.4038/engineer.v44i4.6955)). The Irrigation Department's 2020/21 Hydrological Annual reports updated IDF curves and Snyder parameters for 15 stations, but **formal adoption of these updates is unconfirmed** ([Irrigation Dept](https://www.irrigation.gov.lk/web/images/Publications/Hydrologyical_Annual_2020-21.pdf)).

These findings bear directly on the researcher's flood frequency web app. Its LP3 and Normal options do not include the GEV and L-moment fits that every recent Sri Lankan FFA study preferred.

### No one has defined or characterised a Sri Lankan flash flood

The events usually cited as flash floods turn out, in the published analyses, to be riverine floods or landslides. The May 2003 Ratnapura flood followed **446 mm in three days and 345 mm in one day**, about a 40-year return period ([JICA](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/12291944.pdf)). In May 2016, 350 mm fell over two days on the Kelani, and inundation peaked on 18 May, driven by upland runoff ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311303566_RAPID_EMERGENCY_RESPONSE_MAPPING_FOR_THE_2016_FLOODS_IN_KELANI_RIVER_BASIN_SRI_LANKA)). Aranayake (17 May 2016, 127 killed) is analysed as a rapid long-runout landslide ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10346-018-1089-7)). The May 2017 PDNA describes "flash floods and landslides" in 15 districts but also reports water standing up to 6 m for 4–10 days, which is a riverine signature ([GFDRR](https://www.gfdrr.org/sites/default/files/publication/sri%20lanka%20v11%20LOW.pdf)).

Catchment-response evidence is fragmentary. Synthetic unit hydrographs for ungauged Victoria–Randenigala–Rantambe sub-catchments **peak within 2–3 h of storm onset** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274634436_Prediction_of_Ungaged_Basins_PUB_in_the_Victoria_-_Randenigala_-_Rantambe_VRR_Sanctuary_of_Sri_Lanka_-_Approach_based_on_Synthetic_Unit_Hydrographs)). A data-driven time of concentration has been derived for the 2,816 km² Kalu ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/hydrology12100264)). The notes found no national flashiness index, lag-time regression or unit-peak comparison. A reported Gin basin peak of 4.7 h after onset could not be traced to a source and should not be used. Existing reviews cover hazard management and disaster statistics, not methods ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364087147_A_Review_of_Flood_Hazard_and_Risk_Management_Strategies_Past_Present_and_Future_Challenges_in_the_South_Asian_Region_Particularly_in_Sri_Lanka)).

| Domain | What exists in Sri Lanka | Flash-flood relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Susceptibility mapping | 1 boosting-ML (Ratnapura), 1–2 LR, several unvalidated AHP | Riverine; static predictors; no upland districts |
| Hydrological/hydraulic modelling | HEC-HMS, HEC-RAS, RRI, MIKE, SWAT, LSTM in large basins | One hourly event model (~300 km² Pitabeddara); none <100 km² |
| Flood frequency | At-site GEV/Gumbel/L-moments; SWAT-based ungauged FFA | No regional or non-stationary FFA |
| Design methods | Ponrajah 1984 IDF; Rational/SCS for <25 km² | Unvalidated against observed small-catchment peaks |
| Definitions/characterisation | Disaster narratives (2003, 2016, 2017) | No definition, thresholds, lag times or flashiness |
| Catchment response | VRR 2–3 h peaks; Kalu Tc | No national index or regionalization |

## Three warning silos leave small steep catchments uncovered

Sri Lanka's warnings are organised by hazard, and flash floods fit none of the categories. The Irrigation Department runs **42 permanent gauging stations that record water level and rainfall hourly** and issues three-tier stage warnings: Alert, Minor Flood and Major Flood ([Irrigation Dept](https://www.irrigation.gov.lk/web/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=27&Itemid=128&lang=en)). Warning bulletins frame lead time as the next 24 to 48 hours ([NewsFirst](https://www.newsfirst.lk/2026/08/03/major-flood-warning-issued-for-kelani-river-areas)), which suits large rivers, not catchments with a lag of a few hours. Station thresholds are republished by third-party monitors, so they should be checked against official Irrigation Department figures ([River monitor](https://rivermonitorsl.homerelieflk.org/)).

DoM issues colour-coded rainfall warnings keyed to amounts such as **more than 100 mm** ([Newswire](https://www.newswire.lk/2026/10/12/red-alert-for-heavy-rain-in-several-parts-of-sri-lanka-on-tuesday/)). One report cites 150 mm for the Amber level, and the official tier definitions are **[unverified]** ([Lanka Newspapers](https://www.lankanewspapers.com/2026/10/17/amber-weather-alert-heavy-rainfall-warning-issued-for-six-districts-across-sri-lanka)). A World Bank/GFDRR hydromet review stated plainly that without high-resolution gridded rainfall "it is impossible for the DoM to provide adequate forecasts and warnings of the likelihood of flash floods" ([GFDRR](https://www.gfdrr.org/sites/default/files/publication/Sri%20Lanka%20Hydromet%20Roadmap.pdf)).

The only operational rainfall-threshold system is NBRO's landslide early warning. It has run since 2007, uses about 325 automated gauges, and sets **Watch/Alert/Evacuation levels at 75/100/150 mm per day, or 75 mm/h for evacuation** ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-16898-7_16)). Research thresholds also exist for landslides. A Badulla intensity–duration (I–D) threshold built from 44 events shows that short bursts (under 2 h) above 54 mm/h, or long events (over 8 h) below 25 mm/h, can trigger failures ([NBRO-hosted PDF](https://nbri.gov.lk/images/GEP_2017122214344045.pdf)). A national I–D curve, I = 14.02 D^−0.30, is reported ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317806492_Recommending_Rainfall_Thresholds_for_Landslides_in_Sri_Lanka)), but its claimed "325 landslides 1999–2018" dataset conflicts with the upload date and may be confused with the 325-gauge count **[unverified]**. A Matara study shows that NBRO's thresholds ignore antecedent rainfall even though multi-day accumulation matters there ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212420920314461)). **No flood or flash-flood rainfall threshold for Sri Lanka was found, and no published skill score (POD/FAR) exists for NBRO's or the Irrigation Department's warnings.** The value of 100 mm/24 h recurs across DoM, NBRO and trend studies as a de facto benchmark, but it has never been validated against flood occurrence.

Sri Lanka has been a member of the South Asia Flash Flood Guidance System (SAsiaFFGS) since its October 2020 launch, with IMD as the regional centre. The system gives threat guidance about 6 h ahead ([IMD](https://internal.imd.gov.in/press_release/20201023_pr_922.pdf)). **The only Sri Lankan evaluation found is a qualitative 2018 newsletter piece by a DoM meteorologist, reporting that the products captured May 2018 events in the south-west** ([HRC FFG Gazette](https://www.hrcwater.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/FFG-Gazette_01-08-18_FINAL.pdf)). The most mature satellite-driven system is the JAXA/ICHARM GSMaP-RRI prototype for the Kalu. In that work, merging real-time gauges with GSMaP "significantly improved" estimates of flood onset and peak ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/w15061199)). Its operational status at the Irrigation Department is unconfirmed.

Satellite rainfall has been evaluated mostly at daily and monthly scales. Across dry, intermediate and wet basins, IMERG ranked best on CSI, FAR and PC. The best CSI was in the Kalu (mean 0.53), wet-zone biases were mostly negative (as low as −55.71% for TRMM-3B42RT), and skill was weaker in the dry zone ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cli10100156)). Sub-daily validation is rare because quality-controlled hourly gauge records are hard to obtain. A diurnal-cycle study had to compare at 3-hourly resolution for that reason, and it placed the IMERG rainfall maximum at about **17:30 local time** in all seasons ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169809523000893)). In the SATREPS landslide project, hourly GSMaP matched gauges less well than a high-resolution model simulation ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-18863-2_6)).

Sub-daily design rainfall shows the same pattern. Satellite IDF curves for 50 locations, built from daily data, work at Badulla and Nuwara Eliya but **severely underestimate at Colombo and Ratnapura** ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666016424001075)). A regional IDF study using cluster analysis and regional frequency analysis defines 11 homogeneous regions ([ASCE](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JHYEFF.HEENG-6167)). The only Huff-like design-pattern study is for Greater Colombo (220 events, minimum inter-event time 6 h) ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313164355_Determination_of_a_Design_Rainfall_Pattern_by_Comparing_with_its_Effect_on_Streamflow_on_Greater_Colombo_Watershed_in_Sri_Lanka)). **There is no national Huff curve set**, which is a specific gap the researcher's existing work already addresses.

Data access will decide how far any study can go. DoM sells data but provides it free to students and approved agencies ([Parliament APR 2023](https://www.parliament.lk/uploads/documents/paperspresented/1723001776071832.pdf)). The CRIP project made DoM data free to the Irrigation Department, DMC and NBRO ([World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/418431641855234812/pdf/Sri-Lanka-Improving-Climate-Resilience-Project.pdf)). A planned network of 120 real-time HMIS stations exists on paper, but whether it was installed is **[unverified]** ([World Bank PAD3190](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/890311590112957289/pdf/Sri-Lanka-Integrated-Watershed-and-Water-Resources-Management-Project.pdf)). DesInventar Sri Lanka has records from 1974 and a separate "flash flood" category ([DesInventar](https://www.desinventar.net/DesInventar/profiletab.jsp?countrycode=lka&continue=y)), but it has known inconsistencies: one 2011 flood episode appears as several DesInventar events against a single EM-DAT event ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41885-019-00052-0)). The number of flash-flood records in DesInventar was not retrieved.

## Global practice conditions thresholds on soil and storm, then regionalizes response

### Definitions converge on six-hour onset and catchments below 500 km²

The only widely used operational definition is time-based. The US NWS defines a flash flood as flooding "beginning within six hours of the causative event" ([NWS](https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=flash)). Kelsch notes that many flash floods begin less than 2 h after the rain, so 6 h is a ceiling ([AMS](https://ams.confex.com/ams/pdfpapers/31246.pdf)). European work adds scale and process. Gaume et al. (2009) compiled **more than 550 events, focused on catchments under 500 km²** ([HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00400570)). Marchi et al. (2010) characterised 25 extreme floods in basins of 9.5–1,856 km², and **more than half of their peak discharges came from post-flood surveys of ungauged streams** ([Padova](https://www.research.unipd.it/handle/11577/2422543)). That survey approach is the key methodological precedent for Sri Lanka's largely ungauged small streams. Borga et al. (2014) stress that flash floods occur at scales conventional monitoring cannot capture ([CNR-IRPI](https://www.irpi.cnr.it/publication/hydrogeomorphic-response-to-extreme-rainfall-in-headwater-systems_-flash-floods-and-debris-flows)). The exact WMO wording ("short duration, relatively high peak") is **[unverified]**. A workable Sri Lankan definition would combine onset within 6 h, a catchment ceiling of about 500 km², and a unit-peak or rise-rate severity criterion. Under it, the main-stem Kelani and Kalu floods remain riverine, while their steep tributaries qualify as flash-flood catchments.

### FFPI and flashiness indices map static potential; few are checked against hydrology

The Flash Flood Potential Index (FFPI) originated with Smith (2003) at the NWS Colorado Basin River Forecast Center. It reclassifies slope, land use, soil and vegetation onto a 1–10 scale ([ESRI Fed UC](https://proceedings.esri.com/library/userconf/feduc12/papers/user/joeceru.pdf)). The commonly quoted equal-weight formula is **[unverified]**. The method has developed in three stages: the original equal weights; expert re-weighting, starting with Brewster (2009), who gave slope and vegetation extra weight; and data-driven weights. In Romania the data-driven stage culminated in DLNN–weights-of-evidence ensembles trained on 481 torrential-event points (K-index 0.97) ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7796316/)). India has a Modified FFPI for the Tawi basin ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/185858904d8e4e3e8d0a30e07242a642)). Sri Lanka has no FFPI study by name; the nearest is a 2014 weighted-overlay model for the upper Gal Oya ([SEUSL KALAM](https://www.seu.ac.lk/researchandpublications/kalam/vol8/Pages%20from%20KALAM%20VIII%20(1)%202014%20%20(Page%201-9.pdf)). Across the applied literature, FFPI is usually validated by ROC against the same kind of inventory it was trained on. It is rarely checked against observed hydrological response. That is a gap Sri Lanka's gauges could fill.

Flashiness metrics fall into two families. The Richards-Baker index is computed from daily flows ([MN Water Research](https://wrl.mnpals.net/taxonomy/term/7373)). Its relationship with catchment area **differs between hydroclimatic regions**, according to Holko et al. (2011) as cited in a 2023 paper ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-science/articles/10.3389/fenvs.2023.1203965/pdf)). Event-scale rise-rate flashiness was defined by Saharia et al. (2017). They regressed it on geomorphic and climatic predictors to map flash-flood severity across the contiguous US and identified six hotspots ([NOAA IR](https://repository.library.noaa.gov/view/noaa/48334)). Their formula and model details are **[unverified]**. For the Indian subcontinent, Nandana, Vegad and Mishra (2025) mapped flash-flood-prone sub-basins ([IAS repository](https://repository.ias.ac.in/142483)). Secondary press reports, not checked against the paper, say hotspots include the Western Ghats and that **about 75% of flash floods arise from extreme rain on saturated soils** ([Vision IAS](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/news-today/2025-07-30/environment/new-study-maps-flash-flood-prone-hotspots-across-the-indian-subcontinent)).

The most transferable tools for regionalizing flash-flood response to ungauged catchments are uncalibrated distributed models. EF5/CREST, which powers the US FLASH system, was **skillful without calibration on basins under 1,000 km²**, and its code and parameters are open ([GMD](https://gmd.copernicus.org/articles/13/4943/2020/)). Envelope curves offer a sample-efficient alternative. Amponsah et al. (2020) found that the exponent of the unit-peak envelope curve barely varies across European climatic regions, while the multiplier does ([CNR-IRPI](https://www.irpi.cnr.it/publication/objective-analysis-of-envelope-curves-for-peak-floods-of-european-and-mediterranean-flash-floods)). The numerical values and venue are **[unverified]**.

### Thresholds have become conditional, but FFGS verification remains patchy

Georgakakos (2006) defined flash-flood rainfall thresholds as depth for a given duration and initial soil moisture ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/222571010_Analytical_results_for_operational_flash_flood_guidance)), and Norbiato et al. (2008) tested soil-moisture-conditioned thresholds in gauged and ungauged Italian basins ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022169408004587)). A 2024 review finds that **sensitivity to antecedent soil moisture falls with rainfall duration, while temporal pattern grows in importance** ([IWA](https://iwaponline.com/wpt/article/19/11/4486/105548/A-literature-review-rainfall-thresholds-as-flash)). ROC optimisation is now standard. Korean generalised thresholds are 42, 32 and 20 mm/h for sub-basins of 22–40, 40–100 and over 100 km² ([NHESS](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/18/171/2018/)). Post-wildfire I30 thresholds rose from 16.0 to 34.5 mm/h over five years, so thresholds can change over time ([NHESS](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/22/361/2022/)).

The most rigorous empirical machinery comes from landslide science. CTRL-T reconstructs triggering events and fits frequentist event-rainfall–duration (ED) thresholds with bootstrap uncertainty ([CNR-IRPI](https://www.irpi.cnr.it/en/focus/how-to-define-rainfall-thresholds-automatically/)). An Indian application found that **satellite rainfall outperformed ground observations for landslide prediction** ([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/3267/2021/)). The notes found no flash-flood (as opposed to landslide) application of CTRL-T.

China has gone furthest in building rainfall pattern into thresholds. One study classifies events as leading, intermediate or delayed by where more than 40% of the rain falls, and its thresholds range from 93 to 334 mm and fall sharply with wetter antecedent soil ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326330474_Rainfall_threshold_determination_for_flash_flood_warning_in_mountainous_catchments_with_consideration_of_antecedent_soil_moisture_and_rainfall_pattern)). Yunnan work derived thresholds for each of five peak-timing patterns ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jfr3.12676)). A dual depth-plus-intensity threshold in Sichuan gave a **46.2 min average lead time** with fewer false alarms ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11069-024-06493-5)). A 2026 paper notes that China's fixed 1-, 3- and 6-h operational thresholds "may not fully accommodate the diverse temporal structures of rainfall events" ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/w18010064)). Crucially, most of these pattern-specific thresholds are **derived from model simulations, not estimated from how observed events responded**.

FFGS is operational worldwide ([BAMS](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/bams/103/3/BAMS-D-20-0241.1.xml)), but published verification is thin. The best large-sample verification covers US NWS FFG for 2006–2010 ([WAF](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/wefo/29/2/waf-d-12-00124_1.xml)). National skill figures (Croatia 84%; Türkiye POD 70% and 55%) come from a secondary source ([FloodList](https://floodlist.com/protection/wmos-flash-flood-guidance-system-improves-forecasts-warnings)). Two results suggest FFG skill depends on storm temporal structure. A gridded-FFG validation found the guidance **worked better for long storms than short ones** ([poster](http://www.lakhankar.com/pubs/Poster_Juan_EPP_Forum_Florida.pdf)). In Mediterranean catchments, uniform FFG thresholds overestimated observed thresholds by 8–30% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.07213)). The only SAsiaFFGS evaluation found covers a single Himachal Pradesh event and is qualitative ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/403537199_Evaluating_the_performance_of_the_Flash_Flood_Guidance_System_A_Case_study_from_Himachal_Pradesh_India)).

### Storm shape matters in models but has not been tested on observed inventories

Model experiments agree that hyetograph shape changes peaks. Zhu et al. (2018) found that rainfall temporal structure contributes little as drainage area grows under dry soil conditions, **except in catchments under 20 km²** ([WRR](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2018WR023550)). In convective flash-flood simulations, short-triangle storms gave the fastest time to peak while long-triangle storms gave the highest peak ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022169424004864)), so the shape that maximises the peak is not the one that rises fastest.

Observational evidence is descriptive or concerns spatial structure. In Patagonian ephemeral streams, the sequence of high-intensity bursts set the number of hydrograph peaks ([WRR](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1029/2024WR037601)). Across 5,534 Eastern Mediterranean floods, storm-core properties predicted peaks better than catchment-mean rainfall ([HESS preprint](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2018-550/hess-2018-550-manuscript-version2.pdf)). Depth and shape are not independent either: **rainfall events become temporally more uniform as storm depth increases** ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022169425004391)). Any test of shape must therefore hold depth constant.

Storm shape also appears to change with warming. Wasko and Sharma (2015) found steeper within-storm intensity distributions at higher temperatures across Australia ([UNSW](https://unsw.edu.au/content/dam/pdfs/unsw-adobe-websites/hydrology/2023-11-04-Nature-Geoscience_Wasko-and-Sharma_June-2015-(1).pdf)). Visser et al. (2023) found storms becoming **increasingly front-loaded, with emphasis on short-duration events in the tropics** ([UniMelb](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1797399-changing-storm-temporal-patterns-with-increasing-temperatures-across-australia)). The notes found no comparable South Asian analysis.

Satellite rainfall is the obvious way to obtain sub-daily shape in Sri Lanka, but its timing is uncertain. IMERG and GSMaP show diurnal phase errors of 1–4 h. Over mainland China both peak 2–4 h early ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368799067_Evaluation_of_GPM-IMERG_Precipitation_Product_at_Multiple_Spatial_and_Sub-Daily_Temporal_Scales_over_Mainland_China)). On tropical Trinidad, IMERG spreads the midday peak and GSMaP places it late ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44288-026-00685-1)). One note identifies this paper's study area as Trinidad; the other could not confirm the study area, so check it before citing. In the Xijiang basin, satellite products **missed more than half of events of 50 mm/day or more** ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169809523000704)). **The notes found no study, in any region, that validates event-level Huff class, peak position or Gini concentration from IMERG or GSMaP against gauges.**

### ML susceptibility is saturated; forecasting, regional FFA and inventories are not

Algorithm-comparison susceptibility mapping is crowded ([DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/db1f5a68e6a342f68587314b0e66a91b)), and its headline scores are under attack. For the Feni (Bangladesh) flash flood, **random splits inflated AUC by 0.06–0.09 relative to spatial blocking**, and an unconstrained learner was physically monotone over only 54.5% of its response surface ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44288-026-00759-0)). A Nepal preprint reports that pseudo-absence sampling matters more than algorithm choice ([Research Square](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10471428)). Forecasting is a different story. LSTMs predicted ungauged US basins with median NSE 0.69, against 0.64 for calibrated SAC-SMA ([JKU](https://research.jku.at/en/publications/toward-improved-predictions-in-ungauged-basins-exploiting-the-pow/)). Google's global model, trained on 5,680 gauges, predicts extreme riverine floods in ungauged basins up to 5 days ahead ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v627y2024i8004d10.1038_s41586-024-07145-1.html)). Hourly flash floods in catchments of 10–500 km² lie outside what these models have demonstrated.

Regional FFA in tropical-monsoon settings is recent. An L-moment study of 26 gauges in north-east Bangladesh produced four homogeneous regions, with **R² of 0.61–0.87** for its ungauged-site regressions ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/w17121771)). A Pilbara study notes that most regional flood estimation equations were built for larger catchments ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/hydrology9100165)). Non-stationary GEV with ENSO/IOD covariates is mature in India. In Hyderabad, **local drivers best explained short-duration rainfall extremes and ENSO/IOD best explained long-duration extremes** ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356299497_Time-varying_Intensity-Duration-Frequency_relationship_through_climate-informed_covariates)). Serinaldi and Kilsby's warning against over-fitting time trends to short records is **[prior knowledge]** and needs checking before citing.

Global flash-flood inventories are hydrometeorological and survey-based. Examples are the US Unified Flash Flood Database ([USGS](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70048422)), EuroMedeFF ([ESSD](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/10/1783/2018/essd-10-1783-2018.pdf)) and China's investigation of 2,138 counties ([NHESS preprint](https://nhess.copernicus.org/preprints/nhess-2019-150/nhess-2019-150.pdf)). The MODIS-based Global Flood Database (913 events at 250 m) favours large, long-lived inundation and systematically misses short flash floods ([GEE catalogue](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GLOBAL_FLOOD_DB_MODIS_EVENTS_V1)). The India Flood Inventory shows how to build one from national weather records: about 89% of its records come from digitised IMD reports ([UCAR](https://impacts.ucar.edu/en/publications/india-flood-inventory-creation-of-a-multi-source-national-geospat/)). News mining is a further option. A Bangladesh news series correlated with satellite inundation at ρ = 0.70 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.14943)), though news-based inventories undercount floods in poorer areas ([HESS review](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2024-146/hess-2024-146-AC3-supplement.pdf)).

South Asian analogues are mostly single-event studies. Pakistan 2022 links an extreme flood to pre-monsoon rainfall 111% above normal that wetted soils beforehand ([npj](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44304-025-00109-z)). Kerala 2018 reports return periods above 500 years over reservoirs, but its peer-reviewed status is unconfirmed ([HESSD](https://hess.copernicus.org/preprints/hess-2018-480/)). Melamchi 2021 highlights informal community warning ([ICIMOD](https://www.icimod.org/article/the-melamchi-flood-disaster/)).

## The gap between global practice and Sri Lankan studies

| Topic | Global state of the art | Sri Lanka status (per notes) | Gap |

| Flashiness | RBI + Saharia event flashiness, regionalized | None | Open |
| Rainfall thresholds | Antecedent- and pattern-conditioned, ROC-optimised, CTRL-T | Landslide only (NBRO, Badulla) | Open for floods |

The table shows that the gaps are linked. Thresholds, flashiness maps and FFGS verification all depend on one missing piece of infrastructure: a flash-flood-specific event inventory with reliable dates, locations and sub-daily rainfall attached. Whichever study comes first will have to build that inventory, and the inventory will then make the later studies cheaper. Two gaps, storm-shape effects on observed events and satellite event-shape fidelity, are open worldwide and not just in Sri Lanka. A Sri Lankan answer to either would therefore travel beyond the island.

## Merging thresholds with storm shape gives the strongest study

### Four candidates scored against novelty, data and fit

| Candidate | Novelty in Sri Lanka | Novelty globally | Data feasibility | Fit to researcher | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Rainfall triggering thresholds, small steep catchments | High (no flood threshold exists) | Moderate (method transfer) | Moderate: needs inventory + hourly rain | Good | Inventory timing/location precision |
| 2. National flashiness / FFPI map regionalized | High (no index exists) | Moderate; higher if validated against hydrology | Moderate: RBI from daily flows; event flashiness only at a few sub-daily gauges | Fair | Few unregulated small gauges; regulated Mahaweli flows |
| 3. Storm-shape controls on response | High | **High (no observational large-sample test found)** | Low alone: needs sub-daily hydrographs in small catchments | **Excellent** (Huff/Gini, IMERG) | Too few gauged small-catchment hydrographs |
| 4. Regional / non-stationary FFA, small catchments | High (none exists) | Low–moderate (mature methods) | Low: small-catchment discharge records scarce, 30–60 yr | Good (FFA app) | Weak flash-flood link; overfitting |

Candidate 3 has the most global novelty and fits the researcher best. Taken alone, though, it needs sub-daily discharge at many small catchments, which Sri Lanka lacks. Candidate 1 needs only *occurrence* (flood or no flood), which the inventory supplies. Combining them turns the shape question into an occurrence question: **given equal depth, duration and antecedent wetness, do front-loaded, concentrated storms trigger flash floods more often, and does adding Huff class, Gini concentration or peak position improve threshold skill?** This works with the data Sri Lanka has.

The notes give a three-part novelty case. First, no Sri Lankan flash-flood threshold exists. Second, CTRL-T-style frequentist ED thresholds have not been applied to flash floods anywhere in the notes. Third, the Chinese pattern-conditioned thresholds are model-derived, so testing shape empirically against an observed inventory would be new even internationally ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/326330474_Rainfall_threshold_determination_for_flash_flood_warning_in_mountainous_catchments_with_consideration_of_antecedent_soil_moisture_and_rainfall_pattern); [MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/w18010064)).

Candidate 2 makes the best second paper because it reuses the inventory. It could compute RBI at Irrigation Department gauges, regionalize it with catchment descriptors, and validate an FFPI layer against gauged flashiness rather than against the training inventory. That hydrological validation is itself a gap internationally.

Candidate 4 is real but weaker as a flash-flood study. Annual maxima of daily discharge in large gauged basins say little about flashiness, and regional IDF already covers the rainfall side ([ASCE](https://ascelibrary.org/doi/10.1061/JHYEFF.HEENG-6167)). Its most valuable piece is a modest one: testing Ponrajah, Rational and SCS estimates against observed small-catchment peaks, adding GEV/L-moments to the researcher's app, and using shape-conditioned design storms. That fits as a design-practice chapter or a paper in *Engineer* / JH:RS, not as the lead study.

### What the existing shape work does and does not supply

The researcher's daily-gauge Huff and Gini analysis describes the shape of **multi-day wet spells**. That makes it a natural antecedent-wetness and storm-build-up covariate, the variable the India and Pakistan evidence says dominates monsoon flash floods ([npj](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44304-025-00109-z)). It cannot resolve the intra-day bursts that trigger flash floods in catchments with 2–3 h response times. That layer must come from IMERG half-hourly data checked against hourly gauges. The 1–4 h timing errors in the literature are as long as the bursts themselves. A sensible design therefore uses coarse, robust shape metrics from satellite data, such as Gini on 3-h aggregates or Huff quartile for events of 12 h or longer. It treats fine peak position as valid only where gauges confirm it. Validating IMERG event shape against Sri Lankan hourly gauges is a publishable first step in its own right, and *J. Hydrometeorology* is the natural venue for it.

### Data sources

The flash-flood inventory can draw on DesInventar's "flash flood" category ([DesInventar](https://www.desinventar.lk/)), numbered DMC situation reports and flood bulletins ([DMC](https://www.dmc.gov.lk/index.php?option=com_dmcreports&view=reports&Itemid=277&limit=0&report_type_id=6&lang=en)), the Irrigation Department warning archive ([Irrigation Dept](https://www.irrigation.gov.lk/web/index.php?option=com_content&view=category&layout=blog&id=35&Itemid=260&lang=en)), and multilingual news mining in Sinhala, Tamil and English, following the IFI and Bangladesh templates. Gauge-threshold exceedances at small-catchment Irrigation Department stations would provide hydrological ground truth.

For rainfall, the sources are hourly records at the Irrigation Department's 42 stations, DoM AWS data (free to students), NBRO's automated gauge network if it can be accessed, and IMERG Final half-hourly with GSMaP-Gauge as a cross-check, since the regional literature disagrees on which product times peaks better ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169809525000754)). Antecedent wetness can come from an antecedent precipitation index built from the daily gauges, plus ERA5-Land or SMAP soil moisture. Catchment descriptors can come from the Copernicus/SRTM DEM, ESA WorldCover and SoilGrids, as in standard FFPI work.

### Methods outline

The first step is to fix an operational definition (onset within 6 h, catchment under about 500 km², wet-zone and central-highland tributaries) and screen the inventory events against it. Events that co-occur with landslides should be kept but flagged, since Aranayake-type events confound the two hazards.

Rainfall events are then extracted at each gauge and IMERG pixel, with sensitivity tests on minimum inter-event time ([MDPI](https://doi.org/10.3390/w18080976)). For each event the analysis computes depth, duration, maximum 1-, 3- and 6-h intensities, Huff quartile, Gini concentration, F50 and normalised time to peak.

A baseline is then fitted: frequentist ED/ID thresholds in the CTRL-T style, with bootstrap uncertainty. Next come logistic or ROC models with depth, duration and antecedent index, with and without shape terms. Skill is compared by TSS, CSI and HSS under spatially and temporally blocked cross-validation, following the Feni critique ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44288-026-00759-0)).

Because uniformity covaries with depth ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022169425004391)), shape effects should also be tested with matched-depth comparisons or partial-dependence analysis. Results should be stratified by antecedent class, climatic zone, monsoon season and catchment size, since Zhu et al. predict the effect concentrates in the smallest, driest-antecedent cases. The final thresholds would be benchmarked against NBRO's 75/100/150 mm and DoM's 100 mm levels, and against SAsiaFFGS threat outputs if DoM or IMD can supply archives. An optional extension is a Visser-style trend test on event shape.

### Risks and mitigations

The biggest risk is inventory precision. DesInventar events can be split or duplicated, and their timing may be only to the day, which blurs the link to sub-daily rainfall. Restricting analysis to events that can be confirmed against DMC bulletins or gauge rises, and reporting a confidence tier for each event, limits the damage.

Data access is the second risk. Hourly gauge records are fee- or request-based and partly on paper, and the HMIS expansion is unverified. Applying early through university channels, which qualify for free DoM data, is essential.

Third, IMERG underestimates wet-zone extremes. Its IDF curves fall short at Ratnapura and Colombo, so gauge merging or quantile mapping is needed before thresholds are transferred to ungauged pixels.

Fourth, there is a real chance of a null result. Under saturated south-west monsoon conditions, depth may swamp shape. That would still answer an open question, and it would contradict the model-based literature in an informative way. The hypothesis and stratification should be framed so that a null in saturated cases and a signal in inter-monsoon convective cases are both reportable.

Fifth, the event sample may be small. The landslide literature shows that thresholds can be fitted from about 44 events, as Badulla did, but bootstrap intervals should be reported.

## Conclusion

Sri Lanka's flash-flood blind spot comes from how warnings and research are organised, not from a lack of data. River-stage warnings, rainfall advisories and landslide thresholds each have their own agency, gauges and literature, and the 2–3 h response of steep tributaries falls between them. That changes what counts as a contribution here. A defensible operational definition and a flash-flood-specific event inventory are research outputs in themselves, because every threshold, flashiness map and FFGS verification depends on them. The same inventory also turns the researcher's existing storm-shape pipeline from a rainfall climatology into a hazard tool.

The more interesting implication is that the main open question is not local. Models say storm shape should matter most in small catchments with dry antecedent conditions. Monsoon evidence says saturated soils dominate. No one has tested which holds on observed events. Sri Lanka has sharp wet-zone/dry-zone and monsoon/inter-monsoon contrasts, which make it a natural experiment for separating the two. The right sequence is to validate IMERG event shape against hourly gauges first, then fit shape-conditioned thresholds stratified by antecedent state, then regionalize flashiness with the same inventory, and to fold design-flood checks into that last step. That order keeps each paper publishable on its own and lets each one reduce the data cost of the next.
